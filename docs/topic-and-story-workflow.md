# Topic selection, fact verification, and storytelling for Scribble Shorts

Research and project workflow, version 1.0 (November 2026). Adapted from the Scribble Shorts production project.

Trend-anchored topic selection is Stage 0. It was added after a channel request for episodes chosen from what is currently trending, rather than generic science facts. Stage 0 below and the batch playbook at the end define that process.

## Editorial direction

Make a viewer immediately recognize something, notice an unexpected result, and want to understand it. Each episode should deliver a discovery that is enjoyable to watch and easy to repeat to somebody else.

The subject can come from daily life, the body, animals, nature, objects, technology, space, or history. Selection starts with the viewer's experience and the specific surprise. An ordinary object can support an extraordinary story. An obscure fact needs an accessible entry point.

Use the story and staging stages for factual discoveries, fictional Scribble comedies and visual challenges. Identify the format in the brief. Apply the verification stages to all material real-world claims and puzzle solutions; identify fictional events and explanatory analogies explicitly in the notes. The reference videos that informed this format model structure, not evidence for their scientific claims. Their shared planning principle is a stable goal carried through a sequence of connected problems and solutions.

The central selection question is **Would a person who has never cared about this subject want to see what happens next?** Follow it with **Will the answer feel worth their time?** Both need a concrete answer before production.

This document combines official YouTube guidance, original research on curiosity and communication, and editorial recommendations for this project. The scorecard, candidate counts, timing ranges, and review stages below are project choices. They should evolve with evidence from the channel. This review examined existing scripts and source material. Channel retention analytics and independent viewer tests remain to be collected.

## What the research supports

### Shorts have several distinct viewer decisions

YouTube describes Shorts discovery through whether viewers choose to watch, how long they stay, average percentage viewed, and enjoyment signals such as likes and post-watch surveys. Distribution also varies with topic interest, competition, and seasonality. This supports assessing an episode's opening, progression, and satisfaction separately. A large potential audience helps only when the particular story holds that audience. [YouTube Shorts search and discovery](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en)

In YouTube's interview with Jenny Hoyos and Shorts product lead Todd Sherman, Hoyos emphasizes an immediate hook, and the discussion favors compact stories and memorable moments. Her first-second advice is a creator practice to test. Apply it by making the subject and anomaly legible as the video begins. [YouTube's Shorts deep dive, January 2025](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/)

### Curiosity benefits from a clear question and meaningful progress

Kang and colleagues found that people spent more resources to obtain answers to trivia questions when they were more curious, and that curiosity related to later recall. For our scripts, pose a question the viewer can understand immediately and has a reason to resolve. The study measured curiosity and memory in controlled tasks, so its application to swipe behavior is an inference. [Kang et al., 2009](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x/)

In an experiment using numerical facts, asking participants to generate predictions increased curiosity relative to generating examples. A short, understandable prediction is therefore worth testing when the reveal is strong. Give the viewer a meaningful choice and reveal the result promptly. [Brod and Breitwieser, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31646002/)

Hsiung and colleagues studied drawings that gradually resolved into recognizable objects. Higher curiosity predicted choosing to keep watching, including when participants could jump to the answer. Information continued to arrive during the wait. For this project, make the middle of a Short a sequence of discoveries. Each beat should let the viewer revise a prediction or understand more of the result. The experiment supports this design hypothesis within a controlled drawing task. [Hsiung et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614840/)

### A compelling question needs a satisfying answer

Fastrich and colleagues distinguished interest before an answer from interest after seeing it. Their trivia study found that interest in the answer was an important predictor of later memory. This motivates a separate payoff review. Assess how interesting the resolved fact feels after the suspense has gone. [Fastrich et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6133257/)

Berger and Milkman found relationships between sharing and emotional arousal, surprise, interest, and practical usefulness in research including New York Times articles and experiments. For Scribble, look for wonder, delight, a satisfying correction, or a useful observation. This research concerns sharing in other settings, so Shorts performance must be tested on the channel. [Berger and Milkman, 2012](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)

### Simplicity also applies to the visuals

A study comparing instructional animations and still images found that decorative animations impaired recall and increased reported workload. The context was learning material. Our application is to give Scribble and each visual change a clear role in the explanation. Use movement to demonstrate, compare, reveal, or direct attention. Leave room to inspect the result. [Decorative animations and cognitive load, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32628527/)

## Why the present workflow needs changing

The existing workflow moves from checking one fact to writing a script. It needs a competitive selection stage, an explicit payoff test, and a way to reject a scientifically sound topic with weak audience appeal.

The scripts show several recurring issues.

| Existing episode | What gives it potential | Where the script loses force | Direction for a future treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peppermint | A familiar sensation the viewer has experienced | The opening already gives much of the answer, then quickly introduces menthol and TRPM8 | Start with the puzzling experience. Demonstrate the cold sensation, then explain the cold signal in ordinary language |
| Paper cuts | A familiar annoyance and a striking experiment | The narration moves into the research setup early. The cucumber cut arrives after most of the explanation | Put the surprising cutting result near the start, then investigate how a sheet can do it |
| Raindrops | A familiar drawing and an easy visual comparison | The invitation to draw takes time, and the reveal has relatively little personal consequence | Show the competing shapes immediately. Use the mechanism to make the surprising shape believable |
| Falling Slinky | A visible outcome that violates a likely prediction | The script has a promising opening, then repeats the stationary-bottom observation before explaining it | Show the release quickly, use one clear comparison, and build the explanation around that exact result |
| Blind spot | Direct participation and an observable personal result | Positioning and viewing instructions delay the demonstration and may be difficult on some screens | Prototype the experiment at actual phone size. Make its setup and instructions earn every second |

These are editorial diagnoses from the scripts. Retention data could support or contradict them. A topic can merit another treatment even when its first execution was weak.

## Stage 0 — Start from a trend anchor

Every episode now starts with an **anchor**: something the audience is already paying attention to this week. The anchor is why someone stops scrolling; the fact is what we tell them once they do. A bare evergreen fact is the fallback for a quiet week, not the default.

A fact can be evergreen in content and still be anchored. "Why ice floats" is a weak brief on its own. Attached to a live game launch, a winter cold snap, or a film's arctic setting, the same physics becomes a reason to watch now.

### What counts as an anchor

| Anchor type | Examples | Typical surge | How it decays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Game release or update | A new GTA, a season drop, a record-breaking launch | 1–3 weeks | Slow tail; strongest in launch week |
| Film or series release | A blockbuster, a streaming premiere, an awards run | 1–2 weeks | Sharp after opening weekend |
| Sport | A final, a transfer, a world record | 48 hours–1 week | Immediate |
| Technology or product | A phone, console, AI model, space launch | 3–10 days | Fast |
| Season, weather or holiday | Heatwave, first frost, eclipse, New Year | Days to weeks | Predictable; can be planned ahead |
| Anniversary or record | A dated discovery, a new world record | 2–5 days | Fast |
| Viral moment or challenge | A clip, a stunt, a phrase | 24–72 hours | Very fast; riskiest |

### Do the trend sweep in chat

Automated discovery is optional: with a configured text provider, `node shorts.mjs plan` can compare candidates. Without one, perform this stage with web search and write the brief by hand. Do not substitute memory of what was trending.

1. **Search for what is live now, with dates.** Check at least two independent signals before committing: current YouTube trending or search suggestions in the subject area, Google Trends or autocomplete, gaming/entertainment/tech news front pages, release calendars, and large community discussion (Reddit, X). Record the date, the link, and what each signal actually showed.
2. **Estimate the decay window.** A launch or release gives roughly one to three weeks of above-normal interest; a single news event gives about 48 hours. If the episode cannot be published inside the window, choose another anchor or drop the trend framing and treat it as evergreen.
3. **Check the catalog first.** `PROJECT.md`, `work/*/episodes.json` and `work/pipeline/*/episode.json`. A trend does not excuse a repeat: the same claim staged differently is still a repeat.
4. **Test the fact without the anchor's name.** If the sentence is not interesting on its own, it is a caption, not an episode.

### Turn the anchor into a fact about the world

The claim must be about reality, not about the trend's gossip or an unannounced rumour. "The next GTA cost two billion dollars" is a rumour to verify or discard; "one game made a billion dollars in three days" is a claim with a publisher's statement behind it.

Prefer facts that:

- can be drawn with the existing diagram templates (`comparison`, `layers`, `process`, `callout`) and the Scribble rig, without new assets;
- remain true and interesting after the surge ends, so the upload does not rot;
- have a primary or institutional source: developer or publisher statements, platform or record-body data, peer-reviewed research, government, museum or university material. Games press, wikis, aggregators and other creators are leads only.

The trend supplies the hook and the timing. It never supplies the evidence. Stage 3 applies unchanged, and a claim sourced only to a fan wiki does not ship.

### Rights and taste

- **No copyrighted material in frame.** No footage, screenshots, box art, logos, UI, character art, music or sound effects from the anchor's intellectual property. Draw a generic stand-in with the Scribble kit: a plain car, a generic city grid, an unmarked phone, a blank map. Naming the game or film in narration and the description is fine; implying endorsement or partnership is not.
- Use a trademark only to identify the subject, never as the episode's brand, thumbnail style or channel identity.
- Avoid anchors built on a death, a crime victim, an unresolved disaster or a tragedy. Avoid leaks, rumours and unannounced products.
- Keep the fact family-friendly and advertiser-safe. The channel publishes publicly under the owner's account.

### Worked shape: a GTA-anchored brief

Anchor: a GTA release window. Two candidates, both still requiring Stage 3 verification.

1. **"One game made a billion dollars in three days — faster than any film had."** The GTA V launch was reported at US$1 billion in three days by its publisher, a figure covered at the time as a record. Drawable as a `comparison`: a banknote stack beside cinema tickets and a disc. Needs the exact publisher statement and a matched box-office comparison set with the same timeframe.
2. **"The game's Florida has no hills because the real Florida has none."** Florida is a carbonate platform with no bedrock mountains; its highest natural point is a low ridge. Drawable with `layers` and a `callout`. Needs a USGS or state geological source, and care not to overstate a fictional map's fidelity.

The money comparison is strongest in launch week. The geology angle is the safer evergreen for the tail of the surge.

## Stage 1 — Generate competing ideas

Start with roughly 12 candidates drawn from the Stage 0 anchors, spread across several subjects. Shortlist about three before investing in full research. These counts are starting defaults. A clearly exceptional idea can move faster, and a weak batch should be replaced.

Look for observable oddities, surprising capabilities, recognizable frustrations, simple experiments, strange consequences of everyday actions, and familiar beliefs with a demonstrable correction. A historical or animal story can qualify through a vivid action and a simple consequence.

For each candidate, write these six lines.

1. The exact claim, including the condition that makes it true.
2. The familiar object, experience, or situation in the opening.
3. What a viewer might reasonably expect to happen.
4. What actually happens and why that difference is interesting.
5. The image, demonstration, or comparison that delivers the payoff.
6. A credible source lead and the main unresolved factual question.

Use viral posts, search results, and other creators as leads. Trace the underlying claim before treating it as established. Check the episode list, then search the exact fact and close variations on YouTube to assess repetition. If reviewing comparable Shorts, record the creator, URL, opening, reveal, publication date, duration, and observed result. Evaluate several examples and each channel's usual performance where available. A single hit gives limited evidence about why it succeeded.

## Stage 2 — Assess audience appeal

Score each shortlisted idea from 0 to 2 on each dimension. Use 0 for weak or unresolved, 1 for workable with a specific revision, and 2 for strong. Write a short reason beside each score.

| Dimension | What a strong idea demonstrates |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate recognition | The opening object or situation makes sense without background explanation |
| Surprise | The result meaningfully changes a plausible expectation |
| Reason to care | There is a recognizable experience, striking consequence, useful discovery, or enjoyable puzzle |
| Visual clarity | The central action and result can be shown clearly on a phone |
| Simplicity | The explanation follows a short causal chain in ordinary language |
| Payoff | The final demonstration or realization feels worth the opening promise |
| Freshness | There is a distinctive fact, angle, test, or visual treatment compared with existing episodes and obvious repetitions |
| Timeliness | The anchor is live this week, and the fact still makes sense to a viewer who finds it after the surge ends |
| Retellability | A viewer could explain the discovery to a friend in one sentence |

Use the total to compare candidates. Select a candidate only when recognition, surprise, visual clarity, simplicity, and payoff are strong enough to describe concretely. Revise or park a candidate with a material weakness in those areas, even if its total is high. Accuracy must pass the next stage independently.

Reject or park ideas whose appeal depends mainly on a technical label, an unfamiliar proper noun, a tiny numerical difference, a definition trick, or a long chain of prerequisites. An intriguing setup with a mundane answer also needs replacement. An exceptional claim should still feel interesting when stated precisely.

## Stage 3 — Verify the claim and its boundaries

Write the precise claim before writing the dramatic version. Verify both the surprising observation and the proposed explanation. Reuters' standards emphasize cross-checking, accurate attribution, and trying to disprove a story. Those practices inform this stage. [Reuters sourcing and accuracy standards](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)

| Verification step | Evidence to record | Revise or hold when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trace the origin | Original study, record, dataset, experiment, or responsible institution's explanation. Save the relevant section, page, figure, or short excerpt | The source trail ends at recycled trivia, an AI summary, or an untraceable assertion |
| Match the exact claim | What the source actually establishes and the narrower wording it supports | The hook expands the source's population, material, condition, or certainty |
| Assess the evidence | Study type, relevant methods, limitations, and whether the result is established or preliminary | A striking observation is being presented as a universal law |
| Cross-check | Independent expert or institutional corroboration where available, with the relationship between sources noted | Multiple pages repeat the same press release or disagree on a material point |
| Search for contradictions | Searches for corrections, retractions, failed replications, exceptions, and competing explanations | A material conflict remains unresolved |
| Audit quantities | Units, conversions, denominator, dates, definitions, and a reproducible calculation for any comparison | The numerical surprise relies on mismatched measurements or missing context |
| Audit the mechanism | Evidence for each causal step and the limits of any analogy | A proposed mechanism is being turned into an established cause |
| Audit the demonstration | Conditions, setup, timing, scale, and likely variability | The planned image makes an effect look broader, longer, larger, or more reliable than the evidence supports |

Aim for a source close to the evidence and an independent corroborating check. Source quality and provenance matter more than a page count. One authoritative record may settle a narrow historical claim. A disputed scientific claim needs deeper investigation. If only an abstract is accessible, record that limit and keep the claim within what was actually checked.

The Science Media Centre recommends identifying study scope, distinguishing association from causation, preserving limitations, and keeping headlines faithful to the evidence. Apply those principles whenever condensing research. Preserve qualifying words when removing them would change what viewers believe. [Science Media Centre reporting guidelines](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/guidelines-for-reporting/)

Watch especially for words such as *all*, *always*, *only*, *first*, *largest*, *proves*, and *causes*. Check the full comparison set and timeframe behind a superlative. Distinguish observed facts, researchers' interpretations, and our explanatory analogies in the research notes.

If the accurate version loses its appeal, choose another fact. If an important caveat needs more explanation than the story can comfortably support, simplify the scope or park the candidate.

For animations, preserve the real sequence of events. Label slow motion, magnification, simulations, or reconstructions where viewers could otherwise mistake them for direct observation. Show the conditions responsible for the effect. A generated illustration provides a visual explanation and needs its own accuracy check against the evidence.

**Decision at this stage** — Record either *verified for the stated scope*, *needs more evidence*, or *rejected*, with a reason. Material unresolved claims stay out of production.

## Stage 4 — Develop the opening and ending together

For a verified candidate, write about five opening options across at least two approaches. Pair each with a first frame, a first action, and the exact payoff it promises. Evaluate the combinations.

| Approach | What the opening offers | What the episode must deliver |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visible contradiction | An ordinary action produces an unexpected result | A clear view of that result and a satisfying explanation |
| Prediction | A small number of recognizable outcomes | A prompt, unambiguous result that matters to the question |
| Familiar sensation or frustration | A personal experience with a puzzling cause | An explanation that changes how the viewer interprets that experience |
| Surprising capability | An object or creature does something unexpected | Evidence of the capability under the stated conditions |
| Extreme comparison | Two quantities or objects differ in a surprising way | An accurate, understandable comparison with matched units and scope |

A complete opening tells the viewer what to look at, gives a specific reason to care, and makes the next moment worth seeing. Start with the relevant action or anomaly. Cut greetings, episode announcements, generic trivia introductions, and promises of an unspecified surprise.

Test the opening at phone size with sound off for visual legibility, then listen without looking for spoken clarity. The image and narration should concern the same question. Give the viewer enough of the result to believe the promise. Further curiosity can come from how it works, which option succeeds, or whether it happens again.

Draft the payoff before the middle. State exactly what the viewer will have seen or understood when the episode ends. Use that sentence to check every line in the script.

When somebody unfamiliar with the topic is available, show only the opening and ask what they expect to happen and what they want answered. After the whole draft, ask what happened, why, and whether the answer felt worth watching. Record their actual response. Until then, describe the check as an editorial review.

## Stage 5 — Build a short story around the fact

Before polishing narration, write the causal beat sequence. Use this spine when the material supports it:

**Visible goal or anomaly → plausible attempt or prediction → observable result → specific obstacle → necessary change or explanation → test → completed goal/payoff.**

Make each new beat necessary because of the previous result. For a simple discovery, use one obstacle and one test. A more involved mechanism can support two or three connected obstacles. Reveal partial answers as the story develops; a specific remaining problem gives the viewer a reason to continue. Avoid arbitrary suspense, repeated statements of the mystery, and invented failures. A scientifically straightforward demonstration does not need an artificial complication.

| Beat-sheet field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Goal or prediction | What Scribble wants to accomplish or expects to happen |
| Action | What he tests, compares, builds, changes or investigates |
| Result or obstacle | The visible information gained and any remaining limitation |
| Causal transition | Why the next beat follows: because, but, or so |
| Narration and visual | The line, the object/action it explains, and time to inspect it |
| Evidence status | Supporting source and scope; mark fiction, analogy or reconstruction |

Keep the goal and important props recognizable through the sequence. Introduce a component or technical idea when the viewer can see why it is needed. Prefer concrete verbs and familiar objects; name the concept after showing what it does when that ordering helps. Give Scribble an active role in finding the answer, with a prediction, attempt, test or response. His reaction should follow the result.

Adapt the spine to the format. A discovery can move from anomaly to control to cause to a changed-condition test. A comedy can move from goal to shortcut to consequence to escalation to reversal. A puzzle can move from a fair visible question to an inspection or elimination to a demonstrated answer. Preserve a genuine opportunity to solve it; avoid padding the reveal with a countdown.

A useful timing sketch for a roughly 25–35 second episode follows. It is one option, not a runtime limit. Some stories resolve sooner; deeper explanations can run longer when every additional beat earns its place. The supplied references run about 52, 73 and 108 seconds. Their lengths do not establish the best duration for this channel. Choose duration from necessary actions and clear delivery, then test it against comparable releases.

| Approximate position | Job of the beat |
| --- | --- |
| First 0–2 seconds | Show the subject and unexpected action or establish the prediction |
| Around 2–6 seconds | Supply the minimum setup and make the promise credible |
| Around 6–15 seconds | Reveal evidence or run a comparison that changes the viewer's expectation |
| Around 15–25 seconds | Explain the central cause through the same objects and action |
| Final seconds | Deliver a clear result, callback, or demonstration that resolves the opening |

Choose a structure that fits the source material.

- **Demonstration** — Predict, observe, explain, repeat with understanding.
- **Familiar mystery** — Recognize the experience, expose the surprising cause, show its consequence.
- **Unexpected capability** — Show the feat, establish its conditions, explain the mechanism, complete the feat.
- **Comparison** — Establish two options, make a prediction, reveal the difference, explain it.
- **Brief event** — Establish a goal or ordinary situation, introduce the surprising event, show what caused it and what followed.

Keep one central question. When the answer arrives early, give the viewer a clear next question supported by fresh evidence. Let the story progress through cause, consequence, contrast, or a revised prediction. Watch for a middle that merely restates the opening or lists background facts.

Start explanations with familiar actions and objects. Introduce a scientific name only when it helps the viewer understand or remember something essential. Read the narration aloud and give the viewer time to inspect the key result. Choose the shortest duration that delivers the promise clearly. Treat the suggested ranges as drafting aids.

Finish with the promised discovery. An optional loop can make a second viewing enjoyable when the ending naturally reconnects to the opening. The first viewing should deliver a complete answer. Add a call to action only when it contributes something the episode needs.

Use a final name or identity reveal only when it rewards the construction the viewer has followed. Other episodes can finish on a successful test, a changed prediction, an emotional resolution or a comic reversal. End once the opening task has been resolved and the result has had time to register.

## Stage 5A — Select and produce the narration

Kokoro `am_michael` at 0.95 speed is the default reference voice. A configured command adapter can use another installed, local or hosted provider. Choose the engine for the episode rather than requiring one provider for every script.

When considering a voice change, compare the same short passage containing the hook, a causal explanation and the ending. Assess clarity on one listen, natural phrasing, pronunciation, expressive fit, continuity with Scribble, pacing, audio quality and cost. An expressive voice must still make the explanation easy to follow. Use existing samples where they settle the choice; avoid automatically buying auditions for each episode.

For a non-Kokoro engine, use its real voice-selection and cost-preflight flow when producing audio, with valid settings and the applicable spending rules. Record the choice and reason in the episode brief. Save engine/model, voice identity, speed/style settings, pronunciation adjustments and sample findings under `work/<episode>/`. Once final narration is generated, align the animation to the actual audio and inspect the critical cause/result timing. Prefer trimming redundant writing to rushing speech. No voice generation is required merely to update or review this workflow.

Keep the existing voice-first preferences: no background music by default, short narration captions on by default, and useful short labels only when the scene has no captions. A provider change does not change those preferences.

## Worked opening directions for existing topics

These are script-development examples, ready for testing in a revised storyboard. Their wording has received editorial review. Viewer preference remains to be measured.

**Falling Slinky** — “I let go. Watch the bottom coil.” Begin with Scribble releasing the visibly stretched spring, then show the stationary bottom against a fixed marker in labeled slow motion. The next question is why it stays there. End when the collapsing coils reach the bottom and the bundle falls. The source describes this specific suspended-and-released setup. [Cross and Wheatland's experiments and models](https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~wheat/slinky/)

**Raindrops** — “Which shape is falling past your window?” Open on a pointed icon, a sphere, and a flattened drop, with the size distinction introduced immediately after the choice. Small drops remain approximately round, while larger drops can flatten as they fall. Use the bun comparison as a visual reveal within that scope. [NASA's explanation of raindrop shapes](https://gpm.nasa.gov/science/anatomy-of-a-raindrop)

**Peppermint** — “Why does water feel colder after a mint?” Put the familiar sip first and connect the explanation directly to the sensation. Explain that menthol can activate a cold-sensitive channel. Keep the receptor identifier in the research notes unless the story needs it. The experiment cited in the current project establishes an important role for TRPM8 using mice, so any detailed claims about human sensations need matching evidence. [Bautista et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17538622/)

These revisions improve the questions and staging. The same selection process should also reject weaker topics and surface stronger candidates across a wider range of subjects.

## Stage 6 — Check an inexpensive draft before production

Create a rough sequence with the actual narration and the key demonstration. Approve it for final production when all of the following are concrete.

- The opening subject and action are immediately legible at phone size.
- The opening creates an understandable expectation or question.
- Each beat advances the same discovery.
- Each major transition follows from a cause, limitation, consequence or changed prediction; the middle supplies progress before the ending.
- The visual sequence remains understandable with narration hidden, and the narration remains coherent without the images. Review these as separate drafting checks, then check their alignment together.
- The explanation can be followed on one listen without specialist knowledge.
- The result is visible long enough to understand.
- The ending fulfills the opening promise.
- Every material claim, including labels and visual implications, matches the research.
- Every intermediate solution and failure is accurate within its conditions. A compelling sequence must not imply a literal history of invention, animal intention, a buildable device or a universal mechanism unless the evidence supports that reading.
- Runtime is earned by necessary beats and inspection time. Remove repeated explanations and unnecessary setup before increasing narration speed.
- The selected voice is intelligible, suits the story, and gives the important actions enough time to register.

Keep Scribble present from the opening through the payoff. Have him perform the experiment, make a prediction, handle an object, or direct attention. Let him settle while the viewer inspects something important. Retain the project's voice-first approach and short informational labels. Frame critical objects and labels clear of the Shorts interface and test their legibility on a phone.

Rework the topic or opening when the draft is weak. Full rendering follows editorial readiness.

## Stage 7 — Learn from published episodes

YouTube defines engaged views as viewers staying past the initial seconds, excluding loops. Stayed to watch describes the proportion that stayed past those initial seconds. Average view duration and average percentage viewed for Shorts are calculated among viewers who stayed. These metrics answer different questions. [YouTube content-performance definitions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en)

Public views count video starts, so compare them alongside engagement measures. YouTube's current explanation distinguishes views from engaged views and says most analytics remain anchored on engaged views. [YouTube's explanation, August 2026](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/engaged-views-youtube-explained/)

For each release, record the subject, opening wording and image, runtime, payoff timestamp, publication time, and intended test. Compare results at consistent ages, starting with approximately 48 hours and seven days. These are practical review points. Revisit an episode if distribution changes substantially later.

| Observed pattern | Hypothesis to investigate | Next revision to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weak stayed-to-watch relative to similar channel Shorts | Topic appeal, unclear first frame, delayed action, or a weak opening promise | Make the outcome legible earlier or select a more compelling premise |
| Good initial interest, then a sharp drop during setup | The setup delays progress or asks too much effort | Remove prerequisites and demonstrate sooner |
| Drop during the explanation | Jargon, too many causal steps, or a narration/visual mismatch | Show the cause with simpler objects and language |
| Drop immediately before or after a predictable reveal | Viewers may feel they already have the answer | Move the result earlier and shorten the remaining explanation |
| Strong average percentage viewed, weak stayed-to-watch | Committed viewers stay, while the opening converts fewer feed encounters | Test the opening without assuming the topic broadly appeals |
| A replay spike | Delight, careful inspection, or confusion | Read nearby comments and inspect the scene before interpreting the spike |
| Strong engagement, modest reach | Audience size, competition, sample size, or distribution may matter | Review comparable episodes and gather more evidence |

Treat these patterns as diagnostic hypotheses. Keep traffic source, duration, video age, and sample size visible when comparing. A raw percentage target offers little guidance without that context. Use the median of comparable channel Shorts as an initial reference and update it as the catalog grows.

YouTube specifically notes that a retention spike can reflect rewatching caused by unclear content. Inspect the graph and scene together. The availability of automatic key-moment highlights depends on the video's eligibility and available data. [YouTube's audience-retention guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)

Creator Gohar Khan describes using retention drops to develop hypotheses and improve subsequent Shorts. Adopt that loop by recording one specific lesson and one next test after each review. Multiple examples can establish a pattern more convincingly than a single result. [YouTube's interview with Gohar Khan](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/how-to-write-youtube-video-titles-gohar-khan/)

```text
Causal beat sheet: action, result/obstacle, why next, narration/visual, evidence status
Intermediate answers and remaining question after each
Final test, callback or reaction that completes the opening task
Chosen runtime and why each additional beat is necessary
Voice engine, selected voice/settings, sample comparison and selection reason
Actual narration timing and visual alignment
Draft review findings and revisions
Viewer-test findings, if available
Final claim and visual accuracy check
After publishing, metrics, interpretation, and next test
```

## Production decision

Proceed when the scoped claim is supported, the premise is immediately understandable, the opening gives a specific reason to keep watching, the middle delivers progress, and the payoff satisfies the promise. The candidate must also be feasible to show clearly with the project's available tools. If any essential part remains weak, revise or replace the idea before rendering.

## Executing the production workflow

The reusable runner is `shorts.mjs`; see [the setup and usage guide](../README.md) and [the storyboard contract](storyboard-schema.md). It uses the bundled HyperFrames composition and Scribble rig, measured Kokoro narration, one frame timeline and either HyperFrames or optional Remotion. The runner's independent final model audit applies to automatically written scripts; imported briefs keep their declared factual review. Neither replaces inspection of the actual rendered explanation.

Keep technical samples in `work/` and finished episodes in `outputs/`. Inspect actual scene boundaries, mechanisms, labels and payoff before recording visual review. `finish` verifies the reviewed file/storyboard/timeline hashes, preserves existing outputs and uses the standing public-upload workflow with processing verification. Optional images/video/voice require configured adapters and applicable cost controls; unavailable video falls back to a diagram or image. Test-only validation artifacts cannot be uploaded.

## Batch playbook — "make N Shorts and push them"

When the operator asks for a batch, for example "make 5 shorts and push them to youtube", run this sequence without asking for scope or permission again. N is whatever they asked for; treat 5 as the default. Publish to the channel and visibility in `shorts.config.local.json`, and only because the batch request itself authorizes publication.

0. **Check the uploader first.** Run `npm run youtube:status` to refresh the token and read the channel. If it fails, run `npm run youtube:auth` before spending time on renders. Otherwise the batch finishes with five videos and nowhere to put them.
1. **Read the catalog.** Collect existing titles, `topicKey`s and aliases from `PROJECT.md` and the saved `episodes.json` records, then list the closest near-matches for each candidate.
2. **Trend sweep.** Stage 0. Produce N+2 anchors so there is something to reject. Write `work/<batch>/trend-scan.md` with dates, signal links, decay windows and two or three fact candidates each.
3. **Score and select.** Stage 2 rubric including timeliness. Across five episodes use at least three different anchor types. Reject anything that needs an asset the kit cannot draw or a claim the sources cannot settle.
4. **Verify.** Stage 3, with exact quotations from authoritative pages. The trend gives the hook; the evidence stands on its own.
5. **Write one brief per episode.** `work/<batch>/ep-01-<slug>.json`, schema v1, unique `id` and `topicKey`, five or six claims, four to six scenes, `testOnly: false`, a named reviewer in the record. Short on-screen labels, runtime roughly 30–40 seconds (the runner warns below 30), one central question each.
6. **Render sequentially.** `node shorts.mjs run --brief "work/<batch>/ep-01-<slug>.json"`. Never use `--allow-repeat` to force a trend through the duplicate check. Fix the brief and re-run; do not hand-patch a render.
7. **Inspect and review each one.** Check decoded frames across every scene for overflow, label legibility, Scribble's presence, the source credit and the factual match between diagram and narration. Then `node shorts.mjs review --id <id> --reviewer "Your name" --notes "<the specific checks performed>"`.
8. **Publish each one.** `node shorts.mjs finish --id <id>`. It copies the export to `outputs/`, appends the episode to `PROJECT.md` and uploads to the configured channel and visibility, then verifies the intended channel, requested visibility and successful processing. If processing is still pending, run `finish` again to poll; never upload a second copy.
9. **Record the batch.** Add one dated section to `PROJECT.md`: titles, anchors, durations, URLs, hashes, the review performed and anything not checked. Report the same table to the user with the watch links.
10. **Leave a test to read later.** Note each episode's hook type, anchor type and intended comparison at 48 hours and seven days, so the next performance review tests a hypothesis rather than a vibe.

**Failure handling.** A failed verification replaces the candidate; it never softens the claim. An uncertain upload is reconciled from `upload.json` and the channel before anything is retried. If a trend dies mid-batch, finish on the remaining anchors instead of shipping a stale hook. If a brief cannot be rendered accurately with the available diagram templates, drop it rather than shipping a rough visual.
